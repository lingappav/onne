# Topic 5 — One Nation, One Election: Federal Balance Under Stress

> *"A federation is a country that has agreed to disagree, in writing. When you stop letting it disagree, you have not improved it — you have ended it."*

## Why it matters to the novel

The Kovind Committee Report (March 2024) and the **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024**, introduced in Lok Sabha on 17 December 2024 — are not abstract policy. They are the *immediate political backdrop* of the world *The President's Rule* is set in. The novel's central crisis — a Governor suspending an elected state — is exactly the *kind* of decision that becomes easier when Parliament has already normalised the centralisation of electoral timelines.

ONOE is the **constitutional theme** of the book. Federalism is the **constitutional question** the book asks. They are inseparable.

## The core argument

"Simultaneous elections" sounds technocratic. The deeper question is who benefits when state-level and central-level political cycles are forcibly synchronised:

1. **National narratives drown out state narratives.** A Kannada voter going to the polls on the same day for Lok Sabha and Vidhana Sabha gets *one campaign* — and it is the national campaign that fills the airwaves.
2. **Regional parties lose their natural rhythm.** A Karnataka party that builds influence between national cycles is denied that breathing room.
3. **The federal balance — already strained — tilts further toward the Centre.** What looks like efficiency is structurally **centralisation**.
4. **The "fixed term" idea breaks Westminster logic.** If a state government falls, what happens? The Kovind Report's answers raise as many questions as they resolve.

## What the Kovind Committee actually said

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Constituted | 2 September 2023 |
| Chair | Former President Ram Nath Kovind |
| Report size | 18,626 pages |
| Consultation period | 191 days |
| Public responses | 21,500+ |
| Public support claimed | 80% in favour |
| Political-party support | 32 of 47 in favour |
| **Constitutional amendments required** | **18** |
| Most critical articles to amend | Article 83 (Lok Sabha duration), Article 172 (state assembly duration) |
| State ratification needed? | **Committee says NO** for Parliament/state-assembly amendments; **YES** for local-body amendments |

This last line — *that 18 constitutional amendments affecting state-government duration can be passed without state ratification* — is the federalism alarm bell. Article 368 procedure, on the Committee's reading, does not require state ratification for these amendments. **A great many constitutional scholars disagree.**

## The federalism question, plainly

India's Constitution is *quasi-federal* — strongly central but with constitutionally protected state autonomy. Three things are at stake in the ONOE debate:

1. **Term protection for state legislatures.** If a Vidhana Sabha can be dissolved early to "sync" with the national cycle, the autonomous timeline of state government is gone.
2. **Pre-term elections become "mid-term" elections that are *deliberately short*.** A government elected after a mid-cycle fall would serve only until the next synchronised election — sometimes 18 months, sometimes 6 months. Why would anyone govern seriously for 6 months?
3. **The Governor's role expands.** Decisions about when to dissolve an Assembly, when to invoke President's Rule, when to recommend re-election — these become more consequential, more often. *This is the precise hinge of The President's Rule's plot.*

## The arguments on each side (steelmanned)

### For ONOE
- Saves cost (₹3,000–5,000 crore per cycle, by some estimates)
- Saves political bandwidth — government governs instead of perpetually campaigning
- Reduces Model Code of Conduct paralysis
- Voter fatigue from frequent elections is real
- Many democracies (US, Sweden, Indonesia) have synchronised cycles

### Against ONOE
- Erodes federalism by subordinating state cycles to central
- Reduces accountability — voters can't punish a central or state government separately mid-cycle
- Disproportionately hurts regional parties
- Raises constitutional questions (Basic Structure doctrine, federalism as a basic feature)
- Practical issues: what happens when a state government falls mid-cycle?
- The 80% public-support figure is contested in methodology

## Three modern questions the novel asks

1. If a Governor knows the next election cycle is two years away regardless of what he does to the current state government, **does that change the calculus of when he uses the rod**?
2. If the synchronisation makes mid-term elections short-tenure affairs, **what kind of politician runs for those?** And what kind of governance emerges?
3. The Constitution's *basic structure* doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) protects federalism. **Is a synchronised electoral cycle an amendment, or a re-foundation?**
