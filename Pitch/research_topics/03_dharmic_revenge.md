# Topic 3 — Dharma-Justified Revenge: The Ethics of Retribution in Indian Thought

> *"There are wars in which the rules permit no quarter — and the killing that follows is not revenge. It is the closing of an account that the gods themselves opened."*
> — paraphrase of a Mahabharata sentiment

## Why it matters to the novel

Every major character in *The President's Rule* must answer one of literature's oldest questions: **when does private hurt become legitimate retribution?** Indian tradition has a richer and stranger answer than the Western retributive-justice frame — built around **dharma-yuddha** (righteous war) and **danda** (lawful punishment).

The novel does not endorse revenge. It asks how a citizen, a politician, or a soldier reasons their way *to* it — and whether the reasoning is dharma or rationalisation in dharma's clothing.

## The core argument

In the Indic tradition, retribution is not the opposite of righteousness. **It is, under specific conditions, righteousness itself.** The conditions are demanding:

1. The wrong must be against *dharma*, not against ego
2. The avenger must act from *duty*, not from pleasure
3. The proportionality must be respected — *tit for tat* is forbidden; *measured restoration* is required
4. The action must be *deniable to the self* — if it gratifies you, it is no longer dharma
5. The cost is borne by the avenger; never by uninvolved parties

When these conditions are met, the action is *dharma-yuddha* — righteous war. When they are not, the same action is *adharma* — sin in the costume of duty.

## The Mahabharata's central claim

The Kurukshetra war is the archetype. Krishna assures Arjuna it is dharma-yuddha because:
- The Kauravas refused conciliation (Sama)
- They refused gifts/compensation (Dana)
- They refused negotiated division (Bheda)
- *Only then* does Danda become dharma

But the same epic shows the doctrine's failure mode: **once the rules are broken (Drona's killing, Karna's killing, Duryodhana's mace-blow below the belt), the entire system collapses**, and even the "righteous" side ends with hands so bloody that Yudhishthira walks to hell to learn what victory cost.

This is the literary engine of the novel's moral spine. Every character is implicitly being asked: *will you stop at conciliation, gift, dissension — or will you escalate to force, and at what cost to your own dharma?*

## The four upayas in the moral architecture

| Upaya | The dharmic threshold |
|---|---|
| **Sama** — conciliation | First. Always. If you have not tried sama, you are not in dharma. |
| **Dana** — compensation | Second. The wrong can sometimes be made whole with restoration. |
| **Bheda** — dissension | Third. Splitting an unjust coalition is preferable to attacking it. |
| **Danda** — force | Last. And the lateness of its use is itself part of its dharma. |

Revenge that **skips** sama, dana, and bheda is *adharma*. Revenge that arrives only after all three have failed is *yuddha*.

## Three modern questions the novel asks

1. The politician whose career is destroyed by a media trial — does dharma permit retribution against the journalist, the source, or the system? Against whom does the calculus permit force?
2. The family that loses someone to a police shooting — at what point does demand for justice cross from sama (conciliation) into danda (force), and who decides?
3. The Governor who acts against an elected state — does he believe himself to be acting in dharma-yuddha? And how does the novel show the reader the gap between his self-perception and the doctrine he thinks he serves?

## Key thinkers and texts

- **Vyasa** — *Mahabharata* (especially Udyoga, Bhishma, Karna, and Shanti Parvas)
- **Krishna in the Bhagavad Gita** — chapters 1–3 (the dharma-of-action argument)
- **Kautilya** — the four upayas in *Arthashastra*
- **Manu** — *Manusmriti*, on the conditions of legitimate punishment
- **B.K. Matilal** — *Ethics and Epics* (the definitive modern philosophical reading)
- **Gurcharan Das** — *The Difficulty of Being Good* (the Mahabharata as ethics manual)
- **Amartya Sen** — *The Idea of Justice* (Western retributive vs Indic frame)
- **Wendy Doniger** — for the comparative-myth lens (use with caution; contested)

## A note on tone

This topic touches contested ground in contemporary India — interfaith readers, dharma-as-political-shorthand, the line between dharma as ethics and dharma as identity. The novel handles it as **philosophical inquiry, not as polemic**. Pitch materials should signal that clearly: this is not a triumphalist reading; it is the *hard* reading, in which dharma is shown to be the most demanding ethical system, not the easiest.
