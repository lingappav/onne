# Research File 04 — Why Anyone Who Comes to Karnataka Will Love Karnataka

> **Core Thesis for the Novel:** Karnataka is not a state. It is a civic argument. The argument runs: that multiple traditions — Shaivite, Vaishnava, Jain, Sufi, secular — can inhabit the same geography and produce something that is distinctly itself, not a dilution of any one tradition. The Vachana movement, the Vijayanagara synthesis, the Mysuru civic compact, the eight Jnanpith awards, the IT revolution that made Bangalore the world's second-largest technology hub — these are not accidents. They are the evidence. *The President's Rule* treats Karnataka as a character with this argument at its spine.

## 1. The Five Pillars of Karnataka's Distinctiveness

### Pillar 1: The Vachana Movement (12th Century CE)
Basavanna (c. 1131–1167 CE) created something that had no parallel in the medieval world: a mass social reform movement expressed entirely in poetry, in the people's own language, arguing for the equality of all human beings before the divine.

The Vachanas are short prose-poems written in Kannada by the Sharanas (devotees of Shiva). They are:
- Not Sanskrit (the language of the elite) but Kannada
- Not temple religion but direct, unmediated devotion
- Not caste-affirming but explicitly caste-subverting
- Not written by priests but by weavers, potters, cobblers, and kings equally

The **Anubhava Mantapa** (Hall of Experience) that Basavanna founded in Kalyana is sometimes called India's first democratic assembly — a space where caste, gender, and occupation were irrelevant to participation. A woman cobbler's vachana carries the same spiritual weight as a Brahmin minister's.

> *"The rich will make temples for Shiva. What shall I, a poor man, do? My legs are pillars, the body the shrine, the head a cupola of gold. Listen, O lord of the meeting rivers, things standing shall fall, but the moving ever shall stay."*
— Basavanna (translated by A.K. Ramanujan)

**Why a visitor loves this:** Because Karnataka produced this in the 12th century and it is still alive. The vachanas are sung in Bangalore drawing rooms, quoted in political speeches, taught in government schools.

### Pillar 2: The Vijayanagara Synthesis (14th–17th Century)
The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646 CE) was the last great Hindu empire of South India, and it was built on a deliberate synthesis:
- Hindu kings who employed Muslim generals and Persian architects
- A capital city (Hampi) that blended Dravidian temple architecture with Indo-Islamic palace forms
- A royal court that patronised Kannada, Telugu, Sanskrit, Tamil, and Persian literature simultaneously
- The empire that protected South India from the Bahmani Sultanate's expansion — not by rejecting Islam but by absorbing and deploying its best military technology

Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) is the supreme figure: poet, military strategist, religious pluralist. His Amuktamalyada is a Telugu poem of extraordinary sophistication written by a Kannada king who considered himself equally a patron of both languages.

**Why a visitor loves this:** Hampi. Thirty-three square kilometres of ruins that are both majestic and intimate. The boulder-strewn landscape, the Tungabhadra river, the Virupaksha temple still operating after 700 years. It is the most beautiful archaeological site in India that most people outside Karnataka don't know about.

### Pillar 3: The Mysuru Civic Compact
The Wodeyar dynasty of Mysore created one of the most administratively advanced princely states in British India. The statistics:
- Mandatory primary education introduced in 1904 — before most of British India
- First hydroelectric power station in Asia at Shivanasamudra (1902)
- The Krishnaraja Sagar dam (1924) — Asia's largest masonry dam at the time
- Sir M. Visvesvaraya — Bharat Ratna, civil engineer, Divan of Mysore — is the civic ideal of Karnataka competence. Engineers' Day in India is his birthday.

The Mysore Dasara is not just a festival. It is the expression of the Mysuru civic compact: that power is exercised in public, ceremonially, in a manner that makes the governed feel included in its spectacle. The procession route, the illumination of the palace, the ten-day public holiday — this is state power performing itself for the people.

**Why a visitor loves this:** Mysuru is the cleanest, most organised, most aesthetically coherent large city in India. It is the ghost of what Indian urbanism can be.

### Pillar 4: The Literary Canon (Eight Jnanpith Awards)
Karnataka holds the record: eight Jnanpith Awards, more than any other state, including any Hindi-speaking state. In a country that worships Hindi, this is a political fact as much as a literary one.

| Year | Recipient | Work |
|---|---|---|
| 1967 | K.V. Puttappa (Kuvempu) | *Sri Ramayana Darshanam* |
| 1983 | V.K. Gokak | *Bharata Sindhu Rashmi* |
| 1988 | U.R. Ananthamurthy | *Samskara* (novel) |
| 1991 | Vinayaka Krishna Gokak (posthumous) | — |
| 1993 | Girish Karnad | *Tughlaq*, *Hayavadana* |
| 1994 | U.R. Ananthamurthy | (second award) |
| 2006 | Girish Karnad | — |
| 2010 | Chandrashekhara Kambara | *Siri Sampige* |

**The significance:** Kuvempu's *Sri Ramayana Darshanam* retells the Ramayana as a humanist epic. Ananthamurthy's *Samskara* asks whether caste tradition can survive contact with modern conscience. Karnad's *Tughlaq* — written in 1964 about a 14th-century Delhi Sultan — is really about Nehruvian idealism colliding with political reality. These are not regional writers. They are writers who happened to write in Kannada, and whose work asks questions that belong to all of India.

### Pillar 5: Bangalore's Pluralism
Bangalore is not Indian Silicon Valley. It is a city whose pluralism is a civic achievement, not an accident.
- The Kannada-speaking majority has coexisted with Tamil, Telugu, Urdu, Malayalam, and now Tulu-speaking communities for 150 years
- The British cantonment, the Maharaja's administrative city, the Tata/Larsen industrial zones, and the IT campus belts are four distinct Bangalores that have never fully merged and have never fully fought
- The Udupi restaurant as civic institution: the vegetarian South Indian restaurant invented in Bangalore by Tulu migrants. Open 6am–10pm, ₹40 a meal, no hierarchy, no reservation required. It is the most democratic dining institution India has produced.

## 2. Best Books on Karnataka

### History and Culture

1. **Kamath, Suryanath U.** — *A Concise History of Karnataka: From Pre-historic Times to the Present* (1982, Archana Prakashana; multiple editions)
   - **The standard history.** Every Karnataka student has read this. Dense but authoritative. Chapter-by-chapter, era-by-era.
   - Available: Scribd, local Kannada bookshops, Vidyapeeth Studies online
   - URL: https://www.scribd.com/document/702130806/Suryanath-U-Kamat-A-Concise-History-of-Karnataka-Archana-Prakashana-1980

2. **Kamath, Suryanath U.** — *Krishnadevaraya of Vijayanagara and His Times* (IBH Prakashana)
   - The Vijayanagara chapter in depth; essential for Hampi.

3. **Nilakanta Sastri, K.A.** — *A History of South India: From Prehistoric Times to the Fall of Vijayanagar* (4th ed., 1976, Oxford University Press)
   - The Pan-South-India context. Karnataka's history cannot be understood without understanding the Chola-Chera-Pandya-Rashtrakuta-Chalukya matrix.

4. **Karmarkar, A.P.** — *Cultural History of Karnataka: Ancient and Medieval* (1947)
   - Free on Archive.org. Old but thorough on the pre-Vijayanagara cultural formations.
   - URL: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.142963

5. **Nagaraj, D.R.** — *Allama Prabhu and the Shaiva Literary Traditions* (in Kannada; English summaries available)
   - The scholarly standard on the Vachana movement's literary and philosophical dimensions.

### Literature

6. **Ananthamurthy, U.R.** — *Samskara* (1965 Kannada; English translation by A.K. Ramanujan, Oxford University Press 1976)
   - **Non-negotiable reading.** A Brahmin is asked whether a fallen Brahmin deserves the last rites. Everything Karnataka is here: caste, tradition, modernity, the Malnad landscape, the impossibility of clean choices.

7. **Karnad, Girish** — *Collected Plays Volume 1* (Oxford University Press India)
   - Contains *Tughlaq*, *Hayavadana*, *Naga-Mandala*. *Tughlaq* alone is worth a month of reading and re-reading.

8. **Ramanujan, A.K.** — *Speaking of Siva* (1973, Penguin Classics)
   - **The vachanas in English.** Ramanujan's translations of Basavanna, Mahadevi, Allama Prabhu, Dasimayya are the most beautiful translations from Kannada into English that exist. This is where to start with the vachana tradition.

9. **Kuvempu** — *Kanooru Subhamma Heggaditi* (translated as *Kanooru Heggadati* — available in Sahitya Akademi edition)
   - Karnataka's greatest novel by its greatest writer. The Malnad landscape as protagonist.

10. **Tejaswi, P. Lankesh and Poornachandra** — *Churumuri* and selected stories
    - The radical literary tradition in Kannada — anti-establishment, rural, savage.

### On Bangalore Specifically

11. **Nair, Janaki** — *The Promise of the Metropolis: Bangalore's Twentieth Century* (2005, Oxford University Press)
    - **The definitive academic history of Bangalore.** How the city became what it is: the Mysore state's planning decisions, the Cantonment heritage, the PSU era, the IT transition.

12. **Bhagat, Chetan** — *2 States* — read this only to know what Bangalore looks like to an outsider. Then argue with it.

13. **Murthy, Sudha** — *How I Taught My Grandmother to Read and Other Stories* (Puffin) — not an academic book but captures the Kannada domestic world that the novel will touch.

## 4. The Novel's Framework (Karnataka as the Third Character)

*The President's Rule* has a protagonist, an antagonist, and a third lead: Karnataka itself. The state is not backdrop. It is a character with:

- **A memory** (Vijayanagara, Basavanna, Visvesvaraya)
- **A wound** (the repeated imposition of central authority, the unification controversy, the language politics of 1956 and after)
- **A civic aspiration** (the Mysuru compact — the idea that power should be legible, ceremonial, accountable)
- **A contemporary anxiety** (what happens to Bangalore's pluralism when the IT economy fragments; what happens to Kannada culture when Hindi hegemony intensifies)

The President's Rule — constitutional suspension of state government — is the specific wound the novel will dramatise. Central authority overrides the democratic mandate of the state. The question the novel asks: does Karnataka have the civic capacity to resist this?
