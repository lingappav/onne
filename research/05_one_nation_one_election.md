# Research File 05 — One Nation, One Election: Efficiency, Federalism, and the Architecture of Consent

> **Core Thesis for the Novel:** ONOE is not primarily an electoral reform. It is a federalism question wearing electoral-reform clothes. The argument for simultaneous elections is that governance suffers when the country is in permanent campaign mode. The argument against is that synchronising state elections to the national cycle subordinates state democratic mandates to the Centre's political calendar. *The President's Rule* dramatises what happens when Karnataka is caught in this synchronisation — and what tools the Constitution provides to prevent or impose it.

---

## 1. What ONOE Actually Proposes

### The Kovind Committee (2023-2024)
- **Formally constituted:** September 2, 2023
- **Chairperson:** Shri Ram Nath Kovind, former President of India
- **Members:** Amit Shah, Ghulam Nabi Azad, N.K. Singh (15th Finance Commission), Dr. Subhash C. Kashyap (former Lok Sabha Secretary General), Harish Salve (Senior Advocate), Sanjay Kothari (former CVC)
- **Report submitted:** March 14, 2024 (to President Droupadi Murmu)
- **Scale:** 18,626 pages; 191 days of consultations

### The Two-Phase Proposal
**Phase 1:** Simultaneous elections for Lok Sabha and all State Legislative Assemblies
**Phase 2:** Local body elections (Panchayats and Municipalities) within 100 days of Phase 1

### Constitutional Amendments Required
The committee identified **18 constitutional amendments** as necessary, most importantly:
- **Article 83** — Duration of Houses of Parliament (would need a fixed term mechanism)
- **Article 172** — Duration of State Legislative Assemblies (currently terminable by dissolution)
- **Article 85** — Dissolution of Lok Sabha by President
- **Article 174** — Dissolution of State Legislatures

The mechanism: a single appointed date from which all terms run, regardless of when they started. If a government falls midterm, the fresh House serves only the remainder of the original term.

### Political Support/Opposition
- **47 political parties** submitted views
- **32 supported** simultaneous elections
- **15 opposed** — including Indian National Congress, AAP, TMC, DMK, CPI, CPI(M)
- Public survey: 80% of respondents supported (though survey methodology was disputed)

## 2. The Four Main Arguments For ONOE

### Argument 1: Governance Suffers in Permanent Campaign Mode
India holds elections somewhere every few months. The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) freezes new policy announcements for 6-8 weeks before each election. A ruling party at the Centre is perpetually restrained from major decisions because some state is always approaching its election.

Evidence cited:
- Between 2014 and 2019, there were 28 state/UT elections = effectively 28 periods of MCC restraint
- Major infrastructure projects delayed in pre-election periods
- Finance Minister unable to announce certain schemes in pre-election states

### Argument 2: Cost Reduction
The Election Commission of India spent ₹4,000 crore on the 2014 Lok Sabha elections; ₹8,000 crore for 2019. State elections add to this figure annually. Simultaneous elections would require one mobilisation cycle rather than continuous deployment.

Counter-evidence: The cost of democracy is not primarily financial. The opportunity cost of poorer elections (less scrutiny, less voter awareness) is larger than the savings.

### Argument 3: Security Force Deployment
Every election requires CRPF deployment. Simultaneous elections would consolidate this demand.

Counter-argument: This is an argument for better security institution capacity, not for fewer elections.

### Argument 4: Voter Fatigue
Low turnout in elections held close together suggests voter fatigue. Consolidation might increase engagement.

Counter-evidence: Indian voter turnout has been consistently rising (2014: 66%, 2019: 67%). The fatigue argument is not supported by the data.

## 3. The Four Main Arguments Against ONOE

### Argument 1: Federalism — The Central Objection
India is a quasi-federal state: states have their own democratic mandates, not derived from the Centre's. A state election is not a smaller version of a national election — it is a *different election* on different issues with a different electorate (same people, different questions).

Forcing state elections to sync with Lok Sabha elections means:
- State issues will be nationalised — voters will vote on Modi vs. opposition rather than CM vs. opposition
- Regional parties will be disadvantaged against national parties (national wave elections benefit parties with national reach)
- The state's own democratic cycle — built on its own politics — is overridden by a national administrative convenience

**The Congress/INC formal critique:** The 18 amendments required would alter the basic structure of the Constitution. Under Kesavananda Bharati (1973), Parliament cannot amend the basic structure. Federalism is part of the basic structure (SR Bommai v. Union of India, 1994).

### Argument 2: The Anti-Defection Problem
If a government falls midterm, fresh elections will be held for the remainder of the term only. This means:
- MLAs will be under enormous pressure not to withdraw support, even if the government is dysfunctional
- The Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule) will become the tool used to *prevent accountability* rather than enforce it
- Coalition politics — which requires negotiation and the credible threat of withdrawal — becomes structurally impossible

### Argument 3: The Hung House Problem
If Phase 1 elections produce a hung Lok Sabha and a hung state assembly simultaneously, who forms the government? The Constitution provides mechanisms for single elections but not for simultaneous institutional deadlocks.

### Argument 4: Administrative Impossibility
India has 32 state/UT legislatures with different terms. Synchronising them requires:
- Either extending the terms of some assemblies beyond their mandate (anti-democratic)
- Or cutting short the terms of others (also anti-democratic)
- The transitional arrangement is a one-time constitutional emergency by another name

## 4. The SR Bommai Connection (The Novel's Hinge)

**SR Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** is the most important Supreme Court judgment on federalism and President's Rule in Indian constitutional history.

**Background:** The Bommai government in Karnataka was dismissed under Article 356 in 1989 by the Rajiv Gandhi government. SR Bommai challenged it. The case went to a nine-judge bench.

**Holding:** 
1. The floor of the assembly — not the President's subjective satisfaction — is the only legitimate test of a government's majority
2. Proclamation of President's Rule is subject to judicial review
3. Secularism and federalism are part of the basic structure of the Constitution

**For the novel:** The SR Bommai case is set in Karnataka. The novel's fictional constitutional crisis echoes this history. Every character who has a memory of 1989 knows how this works. The legal arguments in the novel's courtroom scenes draw directly on Bommai.

**Why ONOE and Bommai connect:** If ONOE requires a constitutional amendment that alters the basic structure (federalism), it runs into the same wall Bommai built. The novel's constitutional crisis can be the specific attempt to use ONOE's transitional provisions as a mechanism for imposing President's Rule in Karnataka.

### On ONOE Specifically

1. **Jha, Prashant** — *How the BJP Wins: Inside India's Greatest Election Machine* (2017, Juggernaut)
   - Context: how the BJP's electoral machinery works and why ONOE serves certain political logics. Not about ONOE directly but essential context.

### On Indian Federalism

3. **Austin, Granville** — *The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation* (1966, Oxford University Press)
   - **The foundational text.** How the Constituent Assembly designed federalism — the intentions, the debates, the compromises. Read before any other constitutional analysis.

4. **Basu, D.D.** — *Introduction to the Constitution of India* (LexisNexis; multiple editions)
   - The standard reference on constitutional provisions. Chapter on Article 356 (President's Rule) is essential.

5. **Seervai, H.M.** — *Constitutional Law of India* (4th ed., 3 vols., Universal Law Publishing)
   - The most authoritative scholarly commentary. Dense but definitive. Article 356 section is the most cited analysis of President's Rule.

6. **Ramachandran, R.** — *Federalism in India* (2003)
   - Surveys the tension between centre-state relations from Independence to the BJP era.

7. **Palshikar, Suhas** — *Indian Democracy: A Citizen's Guide* (2017, Speaking Tiger)
   - Accessible analysis of how Indian elections actually work, including the federalism dimension.

### International Comparative

8. **Lijphart, Arend** — *Patterns of Democracy: Government Forms and Performance in 36 Countries* (2nd ed., 2012, Yale University Press)
   - The comparative democratic theory framework. India is one of his case studies; his analysis of majoritarian vs. consensus democracy is directly applicable to ONOE.

## 8. The Novel's Framework (Karnataka in the ONOE Trap)

**The constitutional scenario the novel exploits:**

1. ONOE's transitional provisions require all state assemblies to be brought to a common election date
2. Karnataka's assembly term is midway; its government has a legitimate majority
3. The Central government finds a pretext to impose President's Rule (Article 356) — dissolving the state assembly under the cover of a "constitutional emergency"
4. The actual motive: synchronising Karnataka's election date to the national cycle (and to a moment when the Central ruling party is strong)
5. The legitimate government fights back through: SR Bommai precedent, Article 32 petition to the Supreme Court, and public political mobilisation

**The federalism question at the heart of the novel:**
> *Is the Centre's administrative convenience a legitimate reason to override a state's democratic mandate? And if so, where does it stop?*
