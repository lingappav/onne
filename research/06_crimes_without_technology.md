# Research File 06 — The Greatest Crimes Done Without Technology

> **Core Thesis for the Novel:** The worst harms in modern India required no new technology. They required only three things: a legitimate authority (government, law, institution), a chain of compliant human beings, and the decision — made consciously, or by default — not to stop. Hannah Arendt called this "the banality of evil." In the Indian context, it is the banality of bureaucratic consent: the file that was processed, the order that was followed, the question that was not asked. *The President's Rule* uses this framework to argue that the constitutional crisis it dramatises is not an exceptional event — it is the routine operating condition of Indian state power, running on paper and procedure, not surveillance cameras.

---

## 1. The Theoretical Framework

### Hannah Arendt — The Banality of Evil
Arendt's observation from the Eichmann trial (Jerusalem, 1961): Adolf Eichmann, who administered the logistics of the Holocaust, was not a monster. He was a bureaucrat. He was following orders. He did not *think* — and that failure to think was itself the crime.

The "banality of evil" thesis:
- Evil of this scale does not require evil people
- It requires ordinary people who have suspended their judgment in favour of role-following
- The chain of command becomes the mechanism of atrocity: each person does only their small part, so no one feels responsible for the whole

**Application to India:** Replace the Nazi chain of command with the Indian administrative chain:
- The Collector who signs the order
- The SP who deploys the police
- The SDM who files the report
- The MLA who makes the phone call
- The Minister who nods
- The file that moves from desk to desk, each signature adding legitimacy, no single signature bearing the full weight

This is how Partition happened. This is how the Emergency happened. This is how Bhopal happened. This is how the 1984 riots happened. No single technological amplifier. Just paper, procedure, and the human capacity to follow instructions.

### Christopher Browning — Ordinary Men
Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) is the book that tested Arendt's thesis empirically. He studied Reserve Police Battalion 101 — middle-aged German men who were given the option to opt out of shooting Jews. Almost none did.

**The India-specific version of the Browning question:** At every stage of India's major administrative crimes, how many people *could* have stopped it? What did they choose?

## 2. The Six Indian Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Partition (1947)
**Scale:** 10–20 million displaced; estimated 200,000–2 million dead (figures disputed)
**Technology used:** None. Trains. Files. Lists. Maps.

**The mechanism:**
- The Radcliffe Commission drew the boundary in five weeks
- Cyril Radcliffe had never been to India; he drew the line in Delhi using maps and census data
- The Punjab Boundary Force (50,000 troops) was overwhelmed; it had been planned for 40,000 displaced people, not 10 million
- District Collectors were given no instructions on how to handle population movement
- The violence was carried out by ordinary people with ordinary weapons — knives, swords, fire

**What the chain of command did:**
- Mountbatten accelerated the timetable from June 1948 to August 1947 — 10 months to 10 weeks
- The decision was administrative. It was made in a room. It was signed. It killed hundreds of thousands.

**Key source:** Yasmin Khan, *The Great Partition*

### Case Study 2: The Emergency (1975–1977)
**Scale:** 150,000+ arbitrarily detained under MISA; forced sterilisation programme (6–8 million in 1976 alone, with documented coercion); press censorship; judicial subordination
**Technology used:** None that was new. The telegraph. The telephone. Government printing presses.

**The mechanism:**
- Cabinet Secretary P.N. Dhar processed the Proclamation of Emergency on the night of June 25-26, 1975
- All-India Radio broadcast Mrs. Gandhi's statement within hours
- State governments received telex instructions to detain opposition leaders
- The sterilisation programme worked through district family planning targets — each Collector had a quota; performance against quota was evaluated
- Sanjay Gandhi's nasbandi (forced sterilisation) drive in UP used municipal workers, school teachers, and police to bring men to camps
- No digital tracking, no surveillance cameras, no facial recognition. Just targets, quotas, and the threat of departmental action for non-performance.

**The bureaucracy's role:**
- IAS officers who refused orders were transferred, not prosecuted
- Most did not refuse

**Key sources:** Coomi Kapoor, *The Emergency: A Personal History*; Dom Moraes, *Mrs. Gandhi*

### Case Study 3: Bhopal (1984)
**Scale:** 3,000–10,000 dead in the first three days (immediate deaths disputed); 500,000 exposed; 150,000+ with permanent injury
**Technology used:** The leak itself (chemical plant failure). But the crime — the regulatory failure, the government complicity, the corporate escape — was entirely administrative.

**The mechanism:**
- Union Carbide had flagged safety concerns internally since 1982; the Bhopal plant was below their own safety standards
- The MP government knew the plant was in a residential area; zoning had been ignored because of employment pressure
- Warren Anderson (UC CEO) was arrested, released on bail (₹25,000), flown out of Bhopal, and never extradited
- The settlement (1989): $470 million — approximately $1,000 per victim. Negotiated by the Government of India *on behalf of* the victims, without the victims' consent, as their legal representative.
- The Bhopal gas relief administration (BGPRA) took decades to distribute compensation; file processing was the mechanism of deprivation.

**The chain of bureaucratic consent:**
- The state government that ignored safety complaints
- The Ministry of External Affairs that facilitated Anderson's departure
- The Attorney General who accepted the settlement
- The Supreme Court bench that approved it
- The district administration that lost the evidence

**Key sources:** Dominique Lapierre & Javier Moro, *Five Past Midnight in Bhopal*; Bridget Hanna, *Bhopal: Unending Disaster, Enduring Resistance*

### Case Study 4: The 1984 Anti-Sikh Riots
**Scale:** 2,733+ killed (official; independent estimates: 3,000–8,000) across Delhi, Kanpur, Bokaro in three days
**Technology used:** None. Voter rolls. Local knowledge. Kerosene. Iron rods.

**The mechanism:**
- Lists of Sikh-owned properties and families were distributed to mobs; these lists came from voter registration records and ration card data held by the state
- Police were ordered to stand down or actively participated in directing mobs to targets
- Congress leaders coordinated the organisation in specific constituencies
- The Delhi Police Commissioner filed no FIRs for three days
- The administration of "justice" afterward: no senior political leader convicted for 37 years until Sajjan Kumar (2018, sentenced to life imprisonment)

**The bureaucratic instrument:**
- Voter rolls = the targeting mechanism
- Police inaction = the permission mechanism
- Post-riot administration = the impunity mechanism
- The Nanavati Commission (2000) named names but its recommendations were not implemented for years

**Key sources:** People's Union for Democratic Rights, *Who Are the Guilty? Report of a Joint Inquiry into the Causes and Impact of the Riots in Delhi from 31 October to 10 November* (1984); Manoj Mitta & H.S. Phoolka, *When a Tree Shook Delhi*

### Case Study 5: Manual Scavenging
**Scale:** As of 2021 surveys: 58,098 manual scavengers identified (government figure); independent surveys suggest 5+ lakh. 817 deaths in sewers and septic tanks between 2019–2023 (official data).
**Technology used:** None. The "technology" is human hands.

**The mechanism:**
- Manual scavenging — the removal of human excrement by hand — has been illegal since the Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act 1993, and further under the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act 2013
- It continues because: municipalities contract it informally; the Dalit community that performs it has no economic alternative; the law has no enforcement mechanism with teeth; the penalty for employing a manual scavenger is ₹50,000 or 1 year — regularly not imposed
- Each sewer death: the contractor is usually untraceable, the municipality files a routine report, the family receives a compensation cheque (₹10–30 lakh if lucky), and the next worker is sent down without gas-detection equipment or a safety rope

**Why this is a crime without technology:**
- The oppression runs on caste hierarchy, employment desperation, and administrative inertia — no algorithm required
- The monitoring failure is a file-processing failure: districts submit false certificates of "manual-scavenging-free" status

**Key sources:** Bezwada Wilson & Gita Ramaswamy, *India Untouched: The Forgotten Face of Rural Poverty*; Human Rights Watch report on manual scavenging (2014)

### Case Study 6: Judicial Pendency
**Scale (as of 2025):** 5 crore+ cases pending in Indian courts; average time to resolution in district courts: 6–7 years; High Courts: 3–4 years; Supreme Court: 1–2 years (for listed matters — many cases wait years to be listed)
**Technology used:** None is the problem. The courts use paper files, stamp paper, manual scheduling.

**The mechanism:**
- 5.09 crore pending cases against a sanctioned strength of 25,000+ judges (actual strength: ~19,000)
- Vacancy rate in High Courts: 30–40% at any given time
- The pendency is not random — it discriminates: a wealthy litigant with senior counsel gets listed faster; a poor litigant with a free legal aid lawyer waits
- Undertrials: 75%+ of India's prison population is *undertrial* — people who have not been convicted of anything, held because the case has not come up

**Why this is a crime:**
- The Constitution guarantees speedy justice (Article 21 — right to life and personal liberty)
- The denial of speedy justice is not the act of any one official — it is the systemic output of budget decisions, appointment processes, court infrastructure investment levels, and the Bar's practice of seeking adjournments
- Every actor in the chain — the judge, the clerk, the lawyer seeking adjournment, the Finance Ministry that underfunds the court system, the Law Ministry that leaves vacancies unfilled — is doing only their part

## 3. Best Books

### Theoretical Foundation
1. **Arendt, Hannah** — *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963, Viking Press)
   - **Non-negotiable.** The book that frames the entire topic. Read it before the case studies.

2. **Browning, Christopher R.** — *Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland* (1992, HarperCollins)
   - The empirical test of Arendt's thesis. Translates directly to the Indian administrative context.

3. **Milgram, Stanley** — *Obedience to Authority* (1974, Harper & Row)
   - The psychology experiments that show why ordinary people obey destructive authority. The theoretical spine of how chains of compliance form.

### India-Specific
4. **Khan, Yasmin** — *The Great Partition: The Making of India and Pakistan* (2007, Yale University Press)
   - **The best single book on Partition.** Particularly strong on the administrative mechanism: how district-level decisions produced mass violence.

5. **Kapoor, Coomi** — *The Emergency: A Personal History* (2015, Penguin Viking)
   - Journalist who lived through the Emergency. First-person testimonies + political history + administrative analysis.

6. **Bose, Sarmila** — *Dead Reckoning: Memories of the 1971 Bangladesh War* (2011, Hurst)
   - On state-authorised atrocity in the 1971 war — the administrative mechanism of military crimes. Controversial but essential.

7. **Lapierre, Dominique and Javier Moro** — *Five Past Midnight in Bhopal* (2001, Warner Books)
   - Narrative nonfiction on Bhopal; readable and devastating. Start here for the Bhopal chapter.

8. **Hanna, Bridget, Ward Morehouse and Satinath Sarangi (eds.)** — *The Bhopal Reader: Remembering Twenty Years of the World's Worst Industrial Disaster* (2005, The Apex Press)
   - Academic. Contains the text of the settlement, the criminal proceedings, and the compensation administration.

9. **Mitta, Manoj and H.S. Phoolka** — *When a Tree Shook Delhi: The 1984 Carnage and Its Aftermath* (2007, Roli Books)
   - The 1984 anti-Sikh riots: investigation into who gave orders, what the police did, and what happened in the courts.

10. **PUDR and PUCL** — *Who Are the Guilty?* (1984)
    - A 72-page report written by civil liberties groups within weeks of the riots. Names names. Still the most important primary document.
    - Available free: pudr.org

11. **Wilson, Bezwada** — selected interviews and SAFAI Karmachari Andolan (SKA) reports
    - Wilson is the Magasaysay-winning organiser of manual scavengers. His testimonies are primary sources.

12. **Mehta, Pratap Bhanu** — *The Burden of Democracy* (2003, Penguin India)
    - The overarching argument about India's democracy — its strengths and its structural incapacities.

## 5. The Novel's Framework (The Chapter's Argument)

The crimes-without-technology chapter in *The President's Rule* makes a single, clean argument:

**The argument:**
> The greatest harms done to Indians in the twentieth century were not done by terrorists, foreign powers, or natural disasters. They were done by the Indian state — through its own administrative machinery, following its own procedures, processing its own files. The state is not merely an instrument that can be used for good or harm; the state is an organism with its own self-perpetuating tendencies. When the organism is directed at its own citizens, the mechanism is bureaucratic, not technological. No algorithm was needed to draw the Radcliffe Line. No surveillance system was needed to find the Sikhs of Trilokpuri. No facial recognition was needed to bring men to sterilisation camps. Paper was enough. The signature was enough. The file moving from desk to desk was enough.

**The Karnataka connection:**
- The President's Rule itself — the constitutional mechanism the novel dramatises — is the most routine example of this phenomenon: a file signed by a President, on the advice of a Cabinet, based on a Governor's report, dissolves a democratically elected government
- The entire process is lawful. It uses existing constitutional provisions. No new technology required.
- The question is not whether it is legal. The question is whether it is just.
