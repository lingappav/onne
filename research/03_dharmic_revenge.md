# Research File 03 — Dharma-Justified Revenge: The Ethics of Retribution in Indian Thought

> **Core Thesis for the Novel:** The Mahabharata does not ask whether revenge is good or bad. It asks: *under what conditions does private injury become legitimate retribution?* The answer is always tortured, always conditional, and always costs the avenger something. The dishonour of Draupadi's disrobing is the legitimate seed; the killing of sleeping soldiers in the night is the poisoned fruit. The novel must hold both ends of this wire without letting go of either.

---

## 1. The Philosophical Architecture

### What Dharma Is (and Is Not)
Dharma in the Mahabharata is not a fixed moral code. It is *contextual* — it is the right action in this situation, for this person, with this history. B.K. Matilal calls this the Mahabharata's "contextual morality": the same act (killing a sleeping man) is adharmic in one context (Ashwatthama killing the Upapandavas) and debated in another (Arjuna refusing to fight kin).

Three levels of dharma operate simultaneously:
1. **Sanatana Dharma** — eternal, universal duties (do not lie, do not steal)
2. **Varnashrama Dharma** — duties specific to one's station (a Kshatriya must fight)
3. **Apaddharma** — duties in extremity: when the normal rules break down, different rules apply

Revenge enters through *apaddharma* — the emergency ethics. When the normal order of justice fails, what is permitted?

### The Four Upayas (Re-read for Revenge)
The four upayas from Dandaniti have a private ethics equivalent:
1. **Sama** (conciliation) — Try to resolve without conflict
2. **Dana** (gift/offer) — Make the adversary a better offer
3. **Bheda** (division) — Separate the adversary from their support
4. **Danda** (punishment) — When all else fails, strike

Revenge is only dharmic — in this schema — when sama, dana, and bheda have been genuinely tried. Bhima's rage is pure danda without the preceding steps. Krishna's strategy is the full four-step sequence applied across 18 years.

## 2. The Mahabharata Cases — The Evidence

### Case 1: Draupadi's Vastraharana (The Disrobing)
- **The injury:** Draupadi dragged by her hair into the Kaurava court, the Pandavas having staked and lost her
- **The vow:** Bhima swears to drink Duhshasana's blood; Draupadi swears not to bind her hair until that blood is shed
- **The verdict on revenge:** The epic presents Bhima's eventual killing of Duhshasana — tearing open his chest on the Kurukshetra battlefield — as fulfilling a legitimate vow. The reader is not invited to find it monstrous.
- **The complexity:** Draupadi, who has kept her hair unbound for 13 years as a daily reminder of the wrong, is the most morally severe character in the epic. Yudhishthira, the dharma-king, is the one who staked her. Which wrong was greater?

### Case 2: Ashwatthama's Night Massacre
- **The injury:** Drona (his father) was killed by a lie — Yudhishthira said "Ashwatthama is dead" (referring to an elephant, not his son)
- **The revenge:** Ashwatthama enters the Pandava camp at night and kills the sleeping Upapandavas (the five sons of the Pandavas)
- **The verdict:** Unambiguously adharmic. He killed innocents. He killed the next generation. Even Duryodhana, dying, is horrified.
- **The complexity:** The trigger for his revenge (the lie) is real. The response is catastrophically disproportionate.

### Case 3: Krishna and the Killing of Duryodhana
- **The injury:** The entire 13-year arc — exile, failed negotiations, the burning of the lac house, Draupadi's disrobing
- **The method:** Bhima's mace blow to Duryodhana's thigh — below the waist, explicitly against the rules of mace combat
- **Krishna's argument:** "All of this is dharma." Bal Gangadhar Tilak (quoted by Matilal): *"Ethics do not circumscribe punitive action."* When the punishment is the only redress for a systematic wrong, the means of punishment are expanded.
- **The complexity:** Yudhishthira weeps. The war has destroyed both sides. What has been won?

### Case 4: Yudhishthira and the Dog (Mokshadharmaparva)
- The non-revenge case: Yudhishthira is the only one who will not abandon the dog on the road to heaven.
- The dog is Dharma personified.
- **The lesson:** The measure of a person is not how they avenge wrongs but how they treat the powerless when there is no advantage in doing so.

## 3. The Scholars — Who to Read

### Primary Scholarly Works

1. **Matilal, B.K.** — *Ethics and Epics: Philosophy, Culture and Religion* (2002, Oxford University Press)
   - **The essential book.** Matilal argues the Mahabharata is India's site of *moral philosophy*, not moral instruction. It does not tell you what to do; it shows you how hard it is to know.
   - Key chapters: "Krsna: In Defence of a Devious Divinity" and "Moral Dilemmas: Insights from Indian Ethics"
   - Matilal's central point: the Mahabharata expresses "internal criticism" of dharma — the tradition questioning itself.

2. **Das, Gurcharan** — *The Difficulty of Being Good: On the Subtle Art of Dharma* (2009, Oxford University Press)
   - **Most readable.** Das spent 8 years re-reading the Mahabharata and writing this book. Each chapter tracks a single character's moral arc.
   - Particularly good on: Yudhishthira's passivity (why does the just man not act?), Draupadi's burning anger (is her fury righteousness or vengeance?), Krishna's consequentialism.

3. **Hiltebeitel, Alf** — *Rethinking the Mahabharata: A Reader's Guide to the Education of the Dharma King* (2001, University of Chicago Press)
   - Academic but penetrating. Argues the Mahabharata was composed as a unified whole with a single theological vision, not assembled randomly.
   - Best for: understanding the dharma-framework as designed, not accidental.

4. **Sutton, Nicholas** — *Religious Doctrines in the Mahabharata* (2000, Motilal Banarsidass)
   - Best systematic treatment of the four upayas, the ethics of war, and the question of when adharmic means serve dharmic ends.

5. **Fitzgerald, James L. (translator)** — *Mahabharata, Volume 7: The Book of the Women; The Book of Peace, Part One* (2004, University of Chicago Press)
   - The Shanti Parva translation is where most of the explicit dharma-philosophy is. Read Fitzgerald's introduction.

6. **Karve, Irawati** — *Yuganta: The End of an Epoch* (1969, Deserted Village; reissued Orient Blackswan)
   - A Pune anthropologist's ferocious reading of the Mahabharata characters as *human beings*, not symbols. Her chapter on Draupadi is one of the greatest pieces of Indian literary criticism.
   - This is the book that treats Draupadi's revenge as a woman's legitimate political claim, not just a symbolic wound.

7. **Doniger, Wendy** — *The Hindus: An Alternative History* (2009, Penguin Press)
   - Contextualises the Mahabharata within the full range of Sanskrit tradition. Useful for the revenge-dharma interplay across different texts.

8. **Roy, Pratap Chandra (translator)** — *The Mahabharata of Krishna-Dwaipayana Vyasa* (1883-1896, 12 volumes)
   - The complete old translation, free on Sacred Texts: sacred-texts.com/hin/maha/index.htm
   - Not scholarly but complete — good for finding specific passages.

## 4. Key Philosophical Texts (Beyond the Mahabharata)

9. **Tilak, Bal Gangadhar** — *Gita Rahasya (Karma Yoga Shastra)* (1915)
   - Tilak's reading of the Bhagavad Gita as primarily a text about *action in the world*, not withdrawal from it. His argument that Krishna's ethics are consequentialist — results determine morality — is the most influential nationalist reading.
   - Matilal quotes Tilak's key line: "Ethics do not circumscribe punitive action."

10. **Bhattacharyya, K.C.** — *Studies in Philosophy* (Indian Council of Philosophical Research)
    - On the Indian tradition of "apaddharma" (emergency ethics) — when normal rules are suspended.

## 6. The Novel's Framework (What the Chapter Must Argue)

The dharmic revenge chapter in *The President's Rule* cannot be a polemic for revenge. It must hold the Mahabharata's own complexity:

**Position A (the trigger is legitimate):**
- The protagonist has suffered a systematic wrong — not a personal slight, but an institutional crime done through lawful channels
- Normal justice is unavailable: the courts are slow, the media is compromised, the bureaucracy is the instrument of the wrong
- Apaddharma applies: when the normal order fails, different rules operate

**Position B (the method corrupts the cause):**
- Every step away from Sama/Dana/Bheda toward Danda risks becoming the very thing being opposed
- Draupadi's legitimate fury produced Ashwatthama's illegitimate massacre — the revenge logic has its own momentum
- The Mahabharata's final image is not triumph: it is the Pandavas abandoning their kingdom and walking into the snow

**The synthesis the novel must reach:**
> *Revenge may be dharmic in its origin and adharmic in its execution. The question is not whether to act but whether the act, when completed, makes you someone you recognise.*
